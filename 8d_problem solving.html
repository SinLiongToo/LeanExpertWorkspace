⏰ Theory Reference: Qualification Criteria for Drift & Shift

⏳ Repeatability % of Tolerance

Represents the portion of measurement error caused by repeated measurements of the same device by a single operator, relative to the specification limits ($USL - LSL$): $$\text{Repeatability \%Tolerance} = \frac{6 \cdot \sigma_{\text{repeatability}}}{USL - LSL} \times 100\%$$

📈 Components of Observed Variance

The total observed variation in a drift/qualification study is a summation of multiple physical variation components: $$\sigma^2_{\text{observed}} = \sigma^2_{\text{repeatability}} + \sigma^2_{\text{reproducibility}} + \sigma^2_{\text{stress-environment}} + \sigma^2_{\text{time}}$$

  • $\sigma^2_{\text{stress-environment}}$: Stress effect (e.g. thermal or electrical stress, Dynamic Stress Analysis). Typically evaluated at a single site using one tester setup.
  • $\sigma^2_{\text{time}}$: Systematic time-based drift/shift (mostly evaluated during Test Program release to ensure stability over time).

⚖️ Intrinsic vs. Extrinsic Variation

  • Intrinsic Variation / Shift (Loop per run): Variation that happens within a device run or dynamic stress analysis (DSA) loop. It represents the device's own internal behavior.
  • Extrinsic Variation (Drift by ECID): Variation that happens between different units (Electronic Chip ID). It represents part-to-part variation or manufacturing offsets.

🎯 Cp vs. Repeatability Relationship

If the entire process variation were due to repeatability alone (i.e. a perfect process measured with a noisy gage): $$\%\text{Repeatability} \approx \frac{100}{C_p}$$ For a robust qualification criterion where , we target of tolerance. This ensures the measurement system consumes a fraction of the tolerance budget.

⚙️ Process Centering Sensitivity

Unlike standard %GRR, these 4 NPI repeatability metrics are normalized to the median ($P_{50}$). This makes them highly sensitive to process shifts and centering.

If the process average drifts closer to 0, these ratios grow rapidly, flagging potential test program issues before product release.

The 8D (Eight Disciplines) Problem Solving Process is a structured team-oriented approach used to identify, correct, and eliminate recurring quality defects in manufacturing, engineering, and service environments. Originally developed by Ford Motor Company, it is now an industry standard globally.

The Eight Disciplines (D1 to D8)

  • D1 - Establish the Team: Assemble a cross-functional group of experts who have knowledge about the process and product.
  • D2 - Describe the Problem: Detail the problem statement using 5W2H (Who, What, Where, When, Why, How, How Many) to specify the boundary conditions of the symptom.
  • D3 - Develop Containment Action: Implement temporary actions to protect customers and stop defective parts from escaping. Track containment tasks on the Kanban board.
  • D4 - Define and Verify Root Cause: Conduct 3x5 Why and Fishbone (6M) analysis to trace physical, escape, and systemic causes.
    *Simulate Gage R&R (MSA) here to ensure data resolution (ndc ≥ 5) before deciding capability.
  • D5 - Choose and Verify Corrective Actions: Propose permanent corrective actions that remove the root cause. Verify their efficacy in theory.
  • D6 - Implement and Validate Corrective Actions: Deploy the action.
    *Use the Distribution Comparison and Significance Testing tabs to run Welch's t-test and F-test. Confirm if B is statistically improved (Sigma Ratio < 1.0, Delta centering on target) with p < 0.05.
  • D7 - Prevent Recurrence: Update control plans, FMEAs, standard procedures (SOPs), and monitor process capability.
    *Confirm standard process control by monitoring capability indices ($C_{pk} \ge 1.33$ or $1.67$) on the Process Cpk tab.
  • D8 - Congratulate the Team: Celebrate the team, share success stories, archive findings, and close the project.

Process capability indices measure how well a manufacturing or engineering process output meets the specifications defined by the customer or design limits.

Standard Cpk (Parametric)

The classical process capability index, $C_{pk}$, assumes the process values follow a normal Gaussian distribution. It is calculated by comparing the process center to the upper and lower specification limits relative to three times the process standard deviation ($3\sigma$).

$$C_{pu} = \frac{USL - \bar{x}}{3\sigma}, \quad C_{pl} = \frac{\bar{x} - LSL}{3\sigma}, \quad C_{pk} = \min(C_{pu}, C_{pl})$$

Why Standard Cpk Fails on Non-Normal Data

When a process is skewed (like Log-normal, Weibull) or multi-modal (due to tool differences), standard $C_{pk}$ will give incorrect predictions. For instance, in a right-skewed log-normal distribution, standard deviation is bloated by the right-hand tail. The mean is also dragged right. This understates process capability on the left and overstates it on the right.

ISO 22514-2 Percentile Method

To bypass distribution assumptions, the ISO 22514-2 standard defines process capability using distribution percentiles rather than the mean and standard deviation:

  • Median ($X_{50\%}$) is used as the process center.
  • $X_{0.135\%}$ is used in place of the lower $3\sigma$ limit (equivalent to $-3\sigma$ in a normal distribution).
  • $X_{99.865\%}$ is used in place of the upper $3\sigma$ limit (equivalent to $+3\sigma$ in a normal distribution).
$$C_{pu,\text{ISO}} = \frac{USL - X_{50\%}}{X_{99.865\%} - X_{50\%}}, \quad C_{pl,\text{ISO}} = \frac{X_{50\%} - LSL}{X_{50\%} - X_{0.135\%}}, \quad C_{pk,\text{ISO}} = \min(C_{pu,\text{ISO}}, C_{pl,\text{ISO}})$$

In process optimization (e.g. before/after comparisons, validation of tool duplicates, or raw material switches), engineers evaluate the differences between two datasets: (baseline) and (trial/optimized).

Delta ($\Delta$) - The Shift in Process Centering

Delta is the physical difference in centering between the two processes. It measures whether the mean or median of the distribution has shifted.

$$\Delta \text{Mean} = \bar{X}_B - \bar{X}_A$$

Sigma Ratio - The Change in Process Variability

The Sigma Ratio measures the change in dispersion. It is the ratio of standard deviations.

$$\text{Sigma Ratio} = \frac{\sigma_B}{\sigma_A}$$
  • If , B is more controlled and has lower variation than A (Process improvement!).
  • If , B is noisier and has larger variation than A (Process degradation!).

CDF Plot vs. Q-Q Plot for Process Diagnostics

When comparing two distributions, overlaid CDF plots and Q-Q plots provide complementary insights:

  • CDF Plot ($x \to p$): Answers "How much probability accumulates up to each value?" It is highly intuitive for comparing specification exceedance rates (defect rates) and viewing the shape directly relative to USL/LSL. However, subtle differences in the distribution tails or shape can be difficult to see when lines are close.
  • Q-Q Plot ($p \to x$): Answers "How do the corresponding quantiles differ?" By plotting the quantiles of Dataset A against Dataset B at identical percentiles, it magnifies differences in shape, scale, and skewness:
    • Identical Distributions: Points lie perfectly on the $y = x$ reference line.
    • Location Shift (Mean Shift): The points form a line parallel to $y = x$ but offset (shifted up or down).
    • Scale Shift (Variance Difference): The points form a straight line with a slope different from 1.0 (steeper or shallower).
    • Skewness/Tail Differences: The points form a curve, bending away from the straight line, highlighting that one distribution has heavier tails or different skewness than the other.

A process capability index ($C_{pk}$) is only as trustworthy as the measurement data used to calculate it. evaluates if the measurement system (gage + operators) has sufficient capability relative to the process variation or tolerance limits.

Repeatability (EV - Equipment Variation)

Repeatability represents the inherent precision of the measurement device itself. It is the variation observed when measures the multiple times. It is modeled as the error standard deviation ($\sigma_{\text{repeatability}}$).

Reproducibility (AV - Appraiser Variation)

Reproducibility represents operator-to-operator differences. It is the variation observed when measure the using the same gage. It is composed of operator main effects plus operator-part interaction:

$$\sigma^2_{\text{reproducibility}} = \sigma^2_{\text{operator}} + \sigma^2_{\text{interaction}}$$

Gage R&R (GRR) and Total Variation (TV)

Gage R&R is the combined variation of repeatability and reproducibility. The Total Variation (TV) observed in measurements is the sum of Gage R&R and true Part-to-Part variation (PV):

$$\sigma^2_{\text{GRR}} = \sigma^2_{\text{repeatability}} + \sigma^2_{\text{reproducibility}}, \quad \sigma^2_{\text{Total}} = \sigma^2_{\text{GRR}} + \sigma^2_{\text{PV}}$$

Device Drift & Shift Qualification

In semiconductor and hardware qualification, devices (identified by Electronic Chip ID - ECID) are monitored across repeated stress runs or loops to evaluate stability.

  • Repeatability % of Tolerance: Calculated using the residual standard deviation ($\sigma_{\text{repeatability}}$) from a two-way ANOVA layout. This removes extrinsic part-to-part offsets and systematic run drift: $$\text{Repeatability \%Tolerance} = \frac{6 \sigma_{\text{repeatability}}}{USL - LSL} \times 100\%$$
  • Overall Max Drift / Shift (Population-Based): Calculated by taking the mean of all devices at each run (the run means $\bar{Y}_{\cdot t}$, representing the population average at each run) and finding the maximum difference between any two run averages: $$\text{Max Drift/Shift} = \max_t(\bar{Y}_{\cdot t}) - \min_t(\bar{Y}_{\cdot t})$$ This represents the population-level systematic drift/shift (e.g. due to stress or temperature), filtering out individual chip offsets and repeatability noise.
  • Drift Significance (p-value): Calculated using a Welch's t-test comparing the device population at Run 1 ($t=0$) against the Last Run ($t=R-1$). It tests whether the average shift of the device population is statistically significant. If $p < 0.05$, it indicates a genuine systematic shift rather than random measurement noise.
  • Variance Components: Observed variance is modeled as a sum of independent physical contributors: $$\sigma^2_{\text{observed}} = \sigma^2_{\text{repeatability}} + \sigma^2_{\text{reproducibility}} + \sigma^2_{\text{stress-environment}} + \sigma^2_{\text{time}}$$
  • Cp vs. Repeatability Limit: Assuming the entire process variation is due to measurement error alone, the relationship is: $$\%\text{Repeatability} \approx \frac{100}{C_p}$$ For a robust qualification where $C_p = 4$, a repeatability limit of is targeted.
  • Test Program (TP) Repeatability & Sensitivity: In NPI phases, median-normalized metrics are used to detect test hardware/software issues:
    • SD / Median: $\left|\frac{SD}{P_{50}}\right| \times 100\%$ (target ≤ 2%)
    • Range / Median: $\left|\frac{\text{Max} - \text{Min}}{P_{50}}\right| \times 100\%$ (target ≤ 5%)
    • Robust Sigma / Median: $\left|\frac{1.4826 \times \text{MAD}}{P_{50}}\right| \times 100\%$ (target ≤ 2%)
    • Drift Repeatability (Rpt): $Rpt = 3 \sigma_{\text{drift}}$ (target ≤ 0.5)

AIAG-VDA Failure Mode and Effects Analysis (FMEA)

FMEA is a structured, systematic method for identifying potential failure modes in a product or process, evaluating their effects, and prioritizing corrective actions to mitigate risks.

1. Risk Evaluation Metrics (Severity, Occurrence, Detection)

  • Severity (S): Evaluates the seriousness of the failure effect on the customer or subsequent processes (1 = No effect, 10 = Hazardous without warning).
  • Occurrence (O): Estimates the likelihood of the cause occurring during the planned process/design lifecycle (1 = Remote chance, 10 = Almost inevitable).
  • Detection (D): Assesses the ability of current controls to detect the failure mode or its cause before reaching the customer (1 = Almost certain detection, 10 = Cannot detect).

2. Action Priority (AP) Rules

Rather than using the traditional Risk Priority Number ($RPN = S \times O \times D$), modern AIAG-VDA standards use the Action Priority (AP) logic matrix to evaluate engineering priorities (HIGH, MEDIUM, LOW):

  • HIGH Priority (Red): The team must identify corrective actions to reduce severity/occurrence or improve detection controls. (e.g. S is 9-10, or S is 8 and O/D are high).
  • MEDIUM Priority (Amber): The team should evaluate current controls and identify potential improvements.
  • LOW Priority (Green): The risk is acceptable; current controls are sufficient.

Quality Statistics Hub Reference

1. The Core Dilemma: Chi-Square vs. Binomial

When working with ultra-low defect rates like PPM (Parts Per Million), engineers often confuse Chi-Square Tests with Binomial Attribute Sampling Plans. While both process discrete data (Pass/Fail), they serve completely different statistical tasks:

  • Poisson/Binomial sampling is used for lot acceptance testing and validation. It determines the minimum sample size ($n$) required to prove a process does not exceed a target PPM threshold at a specified Confidence Level ($CL$) under a given number of allowed defects ($c$).
  • Chi-Square tests are used for comparative testing. They evaluate whether the difference in defect rates between two independent samples is statistically significant or merely due to random sampling noise.

2. Mathematical Formulas

  • Chi-Square Realized Confidence Level: Unlike Poisson sampling which takes desired confidence as an input, Chi-Square outputs a p-value dynamically based on observed drift. The achieved Confidence Level ($CL$) is explicitly calculated as: $$\text{Realized CL} = (1 - p\text{-value}) \times 100\%$$
  • The Attribute Sampling Formula (Binomial & Poisson): To confirm that a process does not exceed a designated probability boundary $p$ at a fixed Confidence Level ($CL$) under zero failures ($c = 0$), we deploy the cumulative binomial limit: $$(1 - p)^n \le 1 - CL$$ For micro-scale variables where $p$ approaches zero (such as PPM boundaries), applying a Poisson approximation transforms this into the clean "Rule of Three": $$n \approx \frac{-\ln(1 - CL)}{p}$$ Where $p = \text{Target PPM} / 1,000,000$. If $CL = 0.95$, then $-\ln(1 - 0.95) \approx 3$, reducing perfectly to: $$n \approx \frac{3}{p}$$
  • The Chi-Square Framework & Feasibility Risk: The standard chi-square test analyzes differences between frequencies across discrete datasets. The algorithm relies on the following framework: $$\chi^2 = \sum \frac{(O - E)^2}{E}$$ Where $O$ is the Observed value in a cell, and $E$ is the Expected frequency calculated via: $$E = \frac{\text{Row Total} \times \text{Column Total}}{\text{Grand Total}}$$ The "E ≥ 5" Feasibility Trap: Because Chi-Square is a continuous curve approximating discrete step counts, it completely breaks down if the Expected Defect frequency $E$ is less than 5. In low PPM cases, unless your sample sizes span into many millions, the calculated test metric becomes highly unstable and invalid.
  • Automated Run Size ($n_B$) Optimization Engine: Operating as an Inverse Run-Size Planner, by fixing Group A's mass production baseline and pre-defining your experimental target error capacity (e.g., aiming for an aggressive zero-failure $d_B = 0$ validation run), the system determines the critical alpha cross-over boundaries: $$\alpha = 1 - CL \implies \chi^2_{\text{critical}} = 3.841 \quad (\text{for } df=1, 95\%)$$ The code dynamically increments $n_B$ from 1 upward, executing full chi-square cross-multiplications step-by-step. It returns the exact minimum sample threshold point where the mathematical variance overcomes random sampling noise to deliver statistical significance: $$\chi^2_{\text{calculated}}(n_A, d_A, n_B, d_B) \ge \chi^2_{\text{critical}}$$

3. Semiconductor Case Study: The TTR Sample Disparity Paradox

Consider the asymmetric parameters queried during tool benchmarking validations:

  • Group A (POR Baseline Tool): $n_A = 500,000,000$ (Full production history), $d_A = 200,000$ defects (Baseline Rate = 400 PPM).
  • Group B (New TTR Tool Scenario): We assume an engineering run that targets 0 defects ($d_B = 0$).
  • Target Goal: Find the automated sample size needed to prove statistical significance at $CL = 95\%$.

the inverse calculator dynamically outputs a mandatory minimum run count of $n_B \ge 9,602$ units.

The Interpretation Paradox: Why does a target defect count of 0 only require 9,602 samples, but if we change Group B to test 50,000 units, the system suddenly restricts us to a maximum of 11 errors?

Because at 9,602 samples with 0 errors, the observed rate is 0 PPM. The mathematical gap between 0 PPM and 400 PPM is so wide that it only takes 9,602 pieces to prove it's not a fluke. However, if your test size expands to 50,000 pieces, you are gathering more evidence; a rate of 11 errors out of 50,000 equals 220 PPM, which requires a much larger data profile to safely separate from the 400 PPM baseline without falling into random variation flags.

4. The Missing Parameter: Why Population Size ($N$) is Absent

In low-PPM environments, the population $N$ is assumed to be infinitely large relative to the sample size ($N > 10n$). Under this condition, the traditional Hypergeometric Distribution simplifies into the Binomial/Poisson model. Mathematically, the probability of encountering an ultra-rare event depends solely on the raw amount of evidence gathered ($n$) and the occurrence rate ($p$), completely independent of how massive the lot size ($N$) is.

The Universal Truth of Low PPM: Whether your factory builds 1 million or 100 million units a month, to prove a 10 PPM defect threshold with 95\% confidence using a strict zero-defect rule ($c = 0$), you must always inspect an identical sample size of exactly 299,574 units.

  • Case A: Center Shift only (Variability unchanged)
    Example: Tool wear or machine positioning drift shifts the average dimension of parts, but the machining spread remains uniform.

    Example: Upgrading to a new generation machine which has both a different zero point and improved mechanical stability.
    Result: Both Welch's t-test and F-test are significant ($p < 0.05$).

Part Variation (σPart) Assumption: 0 vs. Calculated

  • Type 1 Gage Study (Gage Capability - Cg/Cgk): Usually performed when qualifying a new gage. We measure a single master part repeatedly. Because there is only one part, there is no part-to-part variation (we assume Part Var is 0), and we evaluate the gage's pure repeatability.

  • Characteristics: Because variances are additive ($\sigma^2_{\text{Total}} = \sigma^2_{\text{Gage R\&R}} + \sigma^2_{\text{Part-to-Part}}$), the individual component percentages (Gage R&R + Part-to-Part) .
  • Concept: It represents the portion of the total process variance (variability energy) caused by measurement error.
  • Acceptance Criteria:
    • < 1%: Acceptable (Excellent)
    • 1% to 9%: Marginally acceptable
    • > 9%: Unacceptable

2. Gage R&R %Study Var (Standard Deviation-Based)

  • Formula: $$\%\text{Study Var} = \frac{\sigma_{\text{Gage R\&R}}}{\sigma_{\text{Total}}} \times 100\%$$
  • Characteristics: Because standard deviations are NOT additive, the component percentages (they will exceed 100% when summed).
  • Concept: It compares the measurement spread ($6\sigma_{\text{GRR}}$) to the total process spread ($6\sigma_{\text{Total}}$) in the same physical units as the parts.
  • Acceptance Criteria:
    • < 10%: Acceptable (Excellent)
    • 10% to 30%: Marginally acceptable
    • > 30%: Unacceptable

The Mathematical Relationship

Since variance is standard deviation squared, %Contribution is the square of the %Study Var ratio:
$$\%\text{Contribution} = (\%\text{Study Var})^2 \times 100\%$$
  • For example, a 30% Study Var (the upper limit of marginal acceptability) is mathematically equivalent to 9% Contribution ($0.30^2 = 0.09 = 9\%$).
  • A 10% Study Var (the upper limit of excellent capability) is mathematically equivalent to 1% Contribution ($0.10^2 = 0.01 = 1\%$).

Because squaring a decimal less than 1 makes it smaller, `%Contribution` will always look much smaller than `%Study Var`.
The total observed variance of a process is the sum of the true part variance and the measurement system variance:
$$\sigma^2_{\text{total, observed}} = \sigma^2_{\text{part, true}} + \sigma^2_{\text{Gage R&R}}$$
When Gage R&R variation is high (e.g., %Study Var > 30%), the observed standard deviation ($\sigma_{\text{total, observed}}$) becomes significantly bloated. Since standard Cpk is inversely proportional to standard deviation:

  • Welch's t-Test verifies if the shift in mean $\Delta = \mu_B - \mu_A$ is statistically significant ($p < 0.05$), proving the center adjustment actually occurred.
