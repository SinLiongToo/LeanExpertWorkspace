The Core Dilemma: Chi-Square vs. Binomial

When working with ultra-low defect rates like PPM (Parts Per Million), engineers often confuse Chi-Square Tests with Binomial Attribute Sampling Plans. While both process discrete data (Pass/Fail), they serve completely different statistical tasks.

Mathematical Formulas & Help Guide

When measuring boundaries in low parts-per-million environments, choosing the exact mathematical architecture ensures validity.

1. Chi-Square Realized Confidence Level

Unlike Poisson sampling which takes desired confidence as an input, Chi-Square outputs a p-value dynamically based on observed drift. The achieved Confidence Level ($CL$) is explicitly calculated as:

$$\text{Realized CL} = (1 - p\text{-value}) \times 100\%$$

2. The Attribute Sampling Formula (Binomial & Poisson)

To confirm that a process does not exceed a designated probability boundary $p$ at a fixed Confidence Level ($CL$) under zero failures ($c = 0$), we deploy the cumulative binomial limit:

$$(1 - p)^n \le 1 - CL$$

For micro-scale variables where $p$ approaches zero (such as PPM boundaries), applying a Poisson approximation transforms this into the clean "Rule of Three":

$$n \approx \frac{-\ln(1 - CL)}{p}$$

Where $p = \text{Target PPM} / 1,000,000$. If $CL = 0.95$, then $-\ln(1 - 0.95) \approx 3$, reducing perfectly to:

$$n \approx \frac{3}{p}$$

3. The Chi-Square Framework & Feasibility Risk

The standard chi-square test analyzes differences between frequencies across discrete datasets. The algorithm relies on the following framework:

$$\chi^2 = \sum \frac{(O - E)^2}{E}$$

Where $O$ is the Observed value in a cell, and $E$ is the Expected frequency calculated via:

$$E = \frac{\text{Row Total} \times \text{Column Total}}{\text{Grand Total}}$$

The "E ≥ 5" Feasibility Trap: Because Chi-Square is a continuous curve approximating discrete step counts, it completely breaks down if the Expected Defect frequency $E$ is less than 5. In low PPM cases, unless your sample sizes span into many millions, the calculated test metric becomes highly unstable and invalid.

4. Industry Case Study: The Chi-Square Power Analysis Trap

Online statistical sites offer calculators to determine sample sizes for Chi-Square via Power Analysis. While mathematically valid for macro academic or clinical research, this logic completely collapses under advanced high-yield semiconductor/electronic frameworks.

  • Macro Setting (High Defect Rates): If you want to detect a change from 5% down to 3% defect rates with 95% confidence and 80% statistical power, a Chi-Square power calculation yields an executable sample size of around 1,500 units per group.
  • Micro Setting (Low PPM Environment): If your engineering line wants to prove a subtle optimization shift from 20 PPM ($0.002\%$) down to 10 PPM ($0.001\%$), inputting these parameters into a Chi-Square power matrix forces a mandatory sample requirements of over 45,000,000 units per group!

Summary Matrix Recommendation: Pulling 45 million units is physically and financially impossible on a live assembly floor. Therefore, when verifying sub-PPM standards, engineers must bypass generic cross-group comparative calculations and instead deploy the Attribute Poisson/Binomial $c=0$ Plan (which drops the needed requirement to a manageable ~300,000 units), or shift parameters to numeric continuous datasets ($C_{pk}$ evaluations).

5. The Missing Parameter: Why Population Size ($N$) is Absent

A frequent point of confusion for engineers is: "Why doesn't the calculator ask for the Total Population Size ($N$) of the production lot?"

In low-PPM environments, the population $N$ is assumed to be infinitely large relative to the sample size ($N > 10n$). Under this condition, the traditional Hypergeometric Distribution simplifies into the Binomial/Poisson model. Mathematically, the probability of encountering an ultra-rare event depends solely on the raw amount of evidence gathered ($n$) and the occurrence rate ($p$), completely independent of how massive the background lot size ($N$) is.

The Universal Truth of Low PPM: Whether your factory builds 1 million or 100 million units a month, to prove a 10 PPM defect threshold with 95% confidence using a strict zero-defect rule ($c = 0$), you must always inspect an identical sample size of exactly 299,574 units.
